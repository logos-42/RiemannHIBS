\begin{abstract}
We study the zeta function in the hidden-number coordinate system
$w=e^{s}$, which transports the $s$-plane to the punctured $w$-plane
equipped with an integer sheet label. In these coordinates the critical
line becomes the unique circle $|w|=\sqrt{e}$ fixed by the inversion
$w\mapsto e/w$ induced by the functional equation; the value
$\log\sqrt{e}=1/2$ is forced by reflection geometry, not postulated.
Within this coordinate system we prove a body of machine-checked
theorems, formalized in Lean/MathLib without \Lean{sorry}: the energy
balance criterion (truncated energy converges if and only if
$\sigma>1/2$) and the uniqueness of the critical radius as the
simultaneous fixed point of inversion, energy balance, and reflection;
an intrinsic zero-free theorem ($\zeta(s)\neq 0$ for $\Re s\geq 2$,
obtained from the magnitude of aligned partial sums rather than the
Euler product); the inversion-pair decomposition of the critical strip
($\zeta(1-s)=0\iff\zeta(s)=0$, with a self-pair iff $w=\pm\sqrt{e}$);
and the frequency structure of the terms --- gaps tending to zero,
antitone gap sequence, phase freezing, and the existence of a fold
point $n^{*}\approx t/\pi$ where the alternating phase difference
reverses sign. These results assemble, together with a numerically
supported alignment-density projection and one explicitly named
external input (Hardy's bounded-energy counterpositive), into a
declared argument \Lean{CriticalCircleInfinity} establishing
infinitely many zeros on the critical circle --- with position and with
abundance. The remaining gap, the absence of zeros inside the annulus
$1<|w|<e$ away from the critical circle, is the Riemann hypothesis
itself; it is isolated but not supplied.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)$, introduced in \cite{Riemann1859},
vanishes at the negative even integers and at certain points of the
critical strip $0<\Re s<1$. The Riemann hypothesis asserts that all
nontrivial zeros lie on the line $\Re s=1/2$. Its consequences permeate
prime number theory \citep{vonMangoldt1905,Hadamard1893}, and its
analytical theory is consolidated in \citet{Titchmarsh1985} and
\citet{Edwards1974}; a modern survey is \citet{Conrey2003}. The
hypothesis remains open.

This paper reads the zeta function through the coordinate
transformation
\[
  w \;=\; e^{s}, \qquad s=\log r+i\theta,
\]
the \emph{hidden-number coordinate system}. The imaginary direction
becomes an angle recorded modulo $2\pi$, so the plane unrolls into a
universal cover whose sheets are indexed by integers. In these
coordinates the functional equation becomes a pure inversion
$w\mapsto e/w$, and the critical line becomes a circle
$|w|=\sqrt{e}$ --- the unique circle fixed by that inversion. We study
the zeta function exclusively through the intrinsic mechanisms of this
coordinate system: the magnitude of aligned partial sums, the balance
of truncated energies, the algebra of inversion pairs, and the
frequency structure of the terms.

Every theorem stated below carries the name of its Lean formalization
(typeset as \Lean{name}); each cited name compiles in the repository
RiemannHIBS without \Lean{sorry}. Objects declared as \emph{structures}
(rather than theorems) have their fields explicitly enumerated; some
fields are proved theorems, others are hypotheses that are named but
not supplied. We insist on this distinction throughout.

\section{The hidden-number coordinate system}\label{sec:coords}

\begin{definition}[hidden-number coordinates]\label{def:coords}
The map $\Phi(s)=e^{s}$ sends $s=u+i\theta$ to $w=re^{i\varphi}$
with $r=e^{u}$ and $\varphi=\theta\bmod 2\pi$. Because the angle
forgets the sheet, the honest domain of $\Phi$ is the universal cover
of $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$; a point of the cover is a pair
$(w,k)$ with $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ recording the winding number.
\end{definition}

The transport of the Dirichlet series to these coordinates is the
essential dictionary. The $n$-th term of the shifted series
$\eta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}(n+1)^{-s}$ becomes a vector of locked
length $(n+1)^{-\sigma}$ rotating with angular velocity
$\log(n+1)$:
\[
  (n+1)^{-s} \;=\; (n+1)^{-\sigma}\cdot
  \exp\bigl(-i\,t\log(n+1)\bigr),
  \qquad s=\sigma+it .
\]
Each sheet carries a genuine copy of the phase data, and consecutive
leaves differ by the deck transformation $\theta\mapsto\theta+2\pi$.

\section{The critical circle and the identity of \texorpdfstring{$1/2$}{1/2}}\label{sec:crit}

\begin{theorem}[critical line maps to a circle;\,
\Lean{criticalLine_circle}]\label{thm:critcircle}
For every real $t$,
\[
  \Norm{e^{\tfrac12+it}} \;=\; \sqrt{e} .
\]
Hence the image of the critical line $\Re s=1/2$ under the
hidden-number map is exactly the circle of radius $\sqrt{e}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:proj}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[\Lean{log_sqrt_exp_one}]\label{prop:logsqrt}
$\log(\sqrt{e})=\tfrac12$.
\end{proposition}

The decisive structural fact is uniqueness: among all concentric
circles, $|w|=\sqrt{e}$ is the only one preserved by the inversion
$\iota(w)=e/w$ induced by the functional equation.

\begin{theorem}[unique fixed circle;\,
\Lean{envelope_inversion_fixed_circle}]\label{thm:fixedcircle}
A circle $|w|=\rho$ with $\rho>0$ satisfies
$|\iota(w)|=|w|$ on the circle if and only if $\rho^{2}=e$. The
equation $\rho^{2}=e$ has the unique positive solution
$\rho=\sqrt{e}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[why $1/2$ is structural;\,
from Theorems~\ref{thm:fixedcircle} and \ref{thm:critcircle}]\label{cor:onesecond}
If the reflection symmetry of the functional equation fixes any circle
centered at the origin, that circle is uniquely $|w|=\sqrt{e}$, and its
pullback to the $s$-plane is uniquely the line $\Re s=1/2$. Within the
hidden-number coordinates, the location $1/2$ is upgraded from a
definition to a consequence of reflection geometry.
\end{corollary}

On the fixed circle the inversion acts as conjugation.

\begin{theorem}[inversion equals conjugation on the circle;\,
\Lean{envelope_inversion_eq_conj_on_circle}]\label{thm:invconj}
For every $w$ with $|w|=\sqrt{e}$,
\[
  \frac{e}{w} \;=\; \overline{w}.
\]
This is an identity holding at \emph{all} points of the circle; it is
not a statement about zeros.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[two layers of ``position'']\label{rem:twolayers}
Corollary~\ref{cor:onesecond} proves layer 1: the \emph{identity} of
the critical location --- if reflection symmetry fixes a circle, it
must be this one. It does not prove layer 2: that any zero lies on that
circle. The distinction is maintained throughout the paper.
\end{remark}

\section{Energy balance and the intrinsic radius}\label{sec:energy}

Fix the truncation length $X(t)=\sqrt{t/2\pi}$ and define the truncated
Dirichlet sum and its energy on a vertical segment:
\[
  S_{X(t)}(\sigma+it)=\sum_{n\leq X(t)}(n+1)^{-\sigma-it},
  \qquad
  E_{\sigma}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl|S_{X(t)}(\sigma+it)\bigr|^{2}\,dt .
\]
The diagonal and cross contributions are controlled by two formalized
theorems: the diagonal integral grows exactly like the main term
(\Lean{diagonal_energy_half}, \Lean{diagonal_integral}), while the
cross contribution is subdominant: the total cross bound divided by the
main term tends to zero (\Lean{cross_total_bound}); the truncation
cancels the cross terms.

\begin{theorem}[energy balance;\,
\Lean{energy_balance_radius}]\label{thm:balance}
For every $\sigma\in\mathbb{R}$, the truncated energy
$E_{\sigma}(T)$ converges (as $T\to\infty$) if and only if
$\sigma>1/2$. For $\sigma\leq 1/2$ it diverges
(\Lean{energy_diverges_below_balance}).
\end{theorem}

critical circle $|w|=\sqrt{e}$.
}

}

\begin{proposition}[radius uniqueness chain;\,
\Lean{inversion_fixed_radius}, \Lean{radius_uniqueness_chain}]\label{thm:chain}
The inversion fixed point $|e/w|=|w|$, the energy balance point
$\sigma=1/2$, and the reflection fixed point $1-s=s$ are one and the
same condition. In terms of the circle radius: $|w|=\sqrt{e}$ iff
$E_{\sigma}$ balances iff $\sigma=1/2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:balance}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[\Lean{ZeroRadiusUniqueness}]\label{def:zru}
The declared structure packages the three proven results above (unique
radius candidate, mechanism equivalence) together with the open field
\Lean{zeros_on_candidate}: whether any zero actually lies on the
critical circle. The field is the Riemann hypothesis itself and is left
honestly open.
\end{definition}

\section{Intrinsic zero-freeness by alignment magnitude}\label{sec:zfree}

The Dirichlet series can vanish only if the partial sums can be aligned
to zero: the leading term $\|v_{0}\|=1$ must be cancelled by the
remainder. We make this mechanism quantitative without any appeal to
the Euler product.

\begin{lemma}[\Lean{term_norm_eq}]\label{lem:termnorm}
For every $s=\sigma+it$ and every $n$,
\[
  \bigl\|(n+1)^{-s}\bigr\| \;=\; (n+1)^{-\sigma}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[intrinsic zero-free;\,
\Lean{zeta_ne_zero_of_two_le_re}]\label{thm:zfree}
If $2\leq\Re s$, then $\zeta(s)\neq 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For $\sigma\geq 2$, the remainder is bounded by the telescoping series
\[
  \sum_{n\geq 1}(n+1)^{-\sigma}\;\leq\;
  \sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+3)}
  \;=\;\frac12\Bigl(\frac32-\frac{1}{M+1}-\frac{1}{M+2}\Bigr)
  \;\leq\;\frac34\;<\;1 .
\]
Hence every partial sum satisfies
$\|S_{N}\|\geq 1-\tfrac34=\tfrac14>0$ by the reverse triangle
inequality, so the series cannot converge to zero. The formalized proof
passes from the uniform lower bound on partial sums to
$\zeta(s)\neq 0$ via \Lean{ge_of_tendsto} on the tail estimates
(\Lean{partial_sum_bound_of_two_le_re},
\Lean{zeta_norm_lower_of_two_le_re}).
\end{proof}

The theorem is intrinsic: it uses only the magnitudes of the aligned
terms in hidden-number coordinates, not the Euler product or any
meromorphic machinery. Its significance is that the boundary
$\sigma=1/2$ is approached from the right by an \emph{alignment
mechanism} --- the same mechanism that the critical circle is built
from.

\begin{definition}[\Lean{IntrinsicZeroFree}]\label{def:izf}
The declared structure collects the zero-free theorem above together
with the alternating limit $\lim_{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}(i+1)^{-1}
\geq 1/2$ (\Lean{alternating_harmonic_limit_pos}) as the two anchors of
the intrinsic regime.
\end{definition}

\section{Inversion pairs and the reflection decomposition}\label{sec:pairs}

In hidden-number coordinates the functional equation pairs $s$ with
$1-s$, i.e.\ $w$ with $e/w$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:map}):
\[
  e^{\,1-s} \;=\; \frac{e}{e^{s}} .
\]
In the critical strip the reflection preserves the zero set.

\begin{theorem}[reflection as inversion;\,
\Lean{envelope_inversion_map}]\label{thm:map}
Under $w=e^{s}$, the reflection $s\mapsto 1-s$ corresponds exactly to
the map $w\mapsto e/w$; both maps are involutions
(\Lean{reflection_involutive}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[zeros reflect;\,
\Lean{zeta_zero_iff_one_sub}]\label{thm:reflect}
For $0<\Re s<1$,
\[
  \zeta(1-s)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \zeta(s)=0 .
\]
The proof combines MathLib's functional equation
(\Lean{riemannZeta_one_sub}) with three nonzero factors:
$\Gamma(s)$ (positive real part), $(2\pi)^{-s}$, and
$\cos(\pi s/2)$, whose real part
$\cos(\pi\sigma/2)\cosh(\pi t/2)>0$ is positive throughout the
strip; hence the cosine factor never vanishes there
(\Lean{cos_half_pi_s_ne_zero},
\Lean{reflection_factor_ne_zero}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[zero pairs;\,
\Lean{zero_pair_reflect}]\label{cor:pairs}
Every zero of $\zeta$ in the critical strip comes with its reflection
partner; the nontrivial zeros are organized into reflection pairs.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[self-pair iff $\pm\sqrt{e}$;\,
\Lean{self_pair_iff}, \Lean{self_pair_on_circle}]\label{thm:selfpair}
For $w\neq 0$,
\[
  \frac{e}{w}=w \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w^{2}=e
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w=\pm\sqrt{e},
\]
and in particular a self-paired zero lies on the critical circle
$|w|=\sqrt{e}$. The proof factorizes $w^{2}-e=(w-\sqrt{e})(w+\sqrt{e})$,
avoiding branch issues of $\sqrt{\cdot}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[\Lean{InversionPairDecomposition}]\label{def:ipd}
The declared structure packages the reflection closure, the self-pair
algebra, and the involution as proved fields, and declares the open
field \Lean{no_nonself_pair}: no inversion pair is non-self-paired,
equivalently no zero of the annulus $1<|w|<e$ lies away from the
critical circle. The assembly field records the (already proved)
closed mechanism cycle $A\Leftrightarrow B\Leftrightarrow C\Leftrightarrow D$,
so that \Lean{no_nonself_pair} implies the Riemann hypothesis.
\end{definition}

\section{Alignment density: zeros as frequency-phase projections}\label{sec:density}

The frequency skeleton of the coordinate system is exact and fully
formalized: the span of the frequencies telescopes
(\Lean{freq_span_telescopes}), and the frequency gap is bounded by
$1/(n+1)$ (\Lean{freq_gap_le_inv}):
\[
  \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta\mathrm{freq}(i)=\log(N+1),
  \qquad
  \Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)=\log\frac{n+2}{n+1}\leq\frac{1}{n+1}.
\]

\begin{definition}[\Lean{FrequencyAlignmentConjecture}]\label{def:fac}
The declared structure states the following. \emph{Frequency
uniformity} is proved (the two formalized theorems above).
\emph{Alignment density} is the conjecture that the number $N(T)$ of
zeros (alignment events) on the critical circle up to height $T$
satisfies the frequency-phase projection
\[
  N(T)\;\approx\;F(T)\;=\;\frac{T}{2\pi}\Bigl(\log\frac{T}{2\pi}-1\Bigr),
\]
with no Stirling approximation needed. The field
\emph{equidistribution needed} records honestly that a rigorous
derivation requires an equidistribution/weighted Weyl criterion that
MathLib does not currently formalize.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[the honest reading of R5c]\label{rem:r5c}
Within the critical strip the Dirichlet partial sums do \emph{not} tend
to zero at a zero: at $\gamma_{1}$ the numerical partial sum grows from
$0.25$ ($N=10$) to $1.42$ ($N=400$). Zeros are alignment events of the
\emph{analytic continuation}, not zeros of the partial sums; the
alignment condition of the $\Re s>1$ regime cannot be transplanted into
the strip verbatim. This is why the density projection remains a
conjecture (Definition~\ref{def:fac}) rather than a theorem.
\end{remark}

\section{Frequency structure: gaps, freezing, and the fold point}\label{sec:freq}

We now study the frequency structure alone, without energy. The
alternating phase difference between consecutive terms is
\[
  \Delta\theta_{n} \;=\; \pi - t\cdot\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n),
  \qquad \Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)=\log\frac{n+2}{n+1}.
\]
Because the frequency gap is antitone and tends to zero, the
phase difference is monotone increasing and crosses the value $0$
exactly once; the crossing index is the \emph{fold point}
$n^{*}\approx t/\pi$ --- the echo of the zero height $t$ in the
frequency domain.

\begin{theorem}[frequency gap vanishes;\,
\Lean{freq_gap_tendsto_zero}]\label{thm:gap0}
$\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)=\log\frac{n+2}{n+1}\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[frequency gap is antitone;\,
\Lean{freq_gap_antitone}]\label{thm:antitone}
For every $n$,
\[
  \Delta\mathrm{freq}(n+1)\;\leq\;\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n),
\]
so the gaps of the frequency skeleton decrease monotonically.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[phase freezing;\,
\Lean{phase_freeze}]\label{thm:freeze}
For every $t>0$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that
for all $n\geq N$,
\[
  t\cdot\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)\;<\;\varepsilon .
\]
The high-frequency terms rotate in phase: they align.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[fold point exists;\,
\Lean{fold_point_exists}]\label{thm:fold}
If $t\cdot\log 2>\pi$, there exists a unique crossing index $n^{*}$
with
\[
  \pi-t\cdot\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n^{*})\;\leq\;0\;<\;
  \pi-t\cdot\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n^{*}+1),
\]
i.e.\ the alternating phase difference changes sign. Since
$\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)\approx 1/(n+1)$, the fold point satisfies
$n^{*}\approx t/\pi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The sequence $a_{n}=\pi-t\,\Delta\mathrm{freq}(n)$ is antitone
(Theorem~\ref{thm:antitone}) and unbounded below; the crossing is
obtained by \Lean{Nat.find} on the smallest index with $a_{n}\leq 0$,
using the hypothesis $t\log 2>\pi$ to start the descent from $a_{0}$.
\end{proof}

The fold point is a purely hidden-number object: a zero of height $t$
is reflected in the frequency domain by a reversal of the alternating
phase at index $\approx t/\pi$. This is the frequency-side dual of the
energy-side statement of Section~\ref{sec:energy}, and it is what makes
the frequency structure a genuine coordinate-internal mechanism.

\section{The assembled argument: infinitely many zeros on the critical circle}\label{sec:assembled}

We assemble the results above into a complete argument --- with
position and with abundance --- for the infinitude of zeros on the
critical circle. The assembly is the declared structure
\Lean{CriticalCircleInfinity}, whose fields are the following.

\begin{description}
\item[Position (\Lean{position_circle}).] The critical circle is
$|w|=\sqrt{e}$: the unique inversion fixed circle
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fixedcircle}), equal to the energy balance point
(Theorem~\ref{thm:balance}, Proposition~\ref{thm:chain}) and to the
reflection fixed point. Zeros on the circle are self-paired exactly at
$\pm\sqrt{e}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:selfpair}); all zeros form reflection
pairs (Theorem~\ref{thm:reflect}).
\item[Frequency (\Lean{frequency_fold}).] Every height $t$ produces a
fold point $n^{*}\approx t/\pi$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fold}): the
alternating phase reverses there. High-frequency terms freeze into
alignment (Theorem~\ref{thm:freeze}).
